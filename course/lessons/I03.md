# I03 · Thinking like a researcher: measured, plausible, contradicted

**Time:** about 4 hours · **You will be able to:** grade claims by evidence, test competing diagnoses, and revise a conclusion without abandoning the research question.

## Why this matters for ClashBot

ClashBot produces many tempting stories. If a policy loses against beatdown decks, perhaps the critic cannot judge those states. If simulator results improve while live play does not, perhaps the engine is solely responsible. Both explanations can sound reasonable before being tested.

`HANDOFF.md` contains a dated critic diagnostic that challenged an earlier beatdown explanation. It reports subgroup measurements and uncertainty, then changes the interpretation. That is a useful example of research progress through correction.

The goal of this lesson is to separate observations from explanations. A learner who can do that can read an experiment report critically even without understanding every neural-network detail. The exercises encode narrow evidence rules and require reports that preserve uncertainty.

## 1. A claim must have a subject and a scope

A **claim** is a statement that can be supported or challenged by evidence. A **hypothesis** is a proposed explanation or prediction to test. “The critic is bad” is too vague: bad at what, on which states, against which comparison, and under which metric?

A clearer hypothesis is: “On this fixed evaluation set, the critic ranks beatdown outcomes worse than other outcomes by a practically important amount.” Now we can identify inputs, groups, metric and a meaningful difference.

```python
claim = {
    "subject": "critic ranking",
    "population": "fixed simulator evaluation",
    "comparison": "beatdown minus other decks",
    "metric": "AUC difference",
}
print(claim["comparison"])
```

It prints `beatdown minus other decks`. The subtraction direction matters. Reversing it reverses the interpretation of a negative number.

A claim about this set is narrower than a claim about all future live matches. Scope should remain attached to the result when it is summarized. Removing it can turn a valid observation into an unsupported universal statement.

## 2. Use three evidence labels carefully

**Measured** means directly supported by an identified observation or calculation. **Plausible** means consistent with what is known but not established by a discriminating test. **Contradicted** means evidence conflicts with the stated claim under its scope.

These labels attach to claims, not to people or entire projects. A report can contain measured counts, a plausible mechanism and a contradicted earlier explanation in adjacent sentences.

```python
claims = [
    ("18 wins in 30 decisive games", "measured"),
    ("better timing caused the gain", "plausible"),
    ("there were no losses", "contradicted"),
]
for text, grade in claims:
    print(grade)
```

It prints `measured`, `plausible`, and `contradicted` on separate lines. The same underlying counts support the first claim and contradict the third. They do not identify the cause of the wins.

Measured does not mean perfectly accurate. A calculation can be reproducible yet based on incomplete or biased data. State the source, filtering rule and denominator. If unknown outcomes are excluded, report their count so a reader can assess the missingness.

Contradicted also does not mean a whole research direction must be discarded. It means this claim should not guide decisions unchanged. A more specific or different mechanism may remain plausible.

## 3. Design a test that separates explanations

A **diagnostic test** distinguishes between competing causes. If losses could result from a weak critic or from execution latency, measuring only the overall loss rate does not separate them. Each explanation needs a prediction that differs from the alternatives.

For critic weakness, compare critic discrimination and error across relevant groups on matched evaluation conditions. For latency, measure the time between observation and executed action. For engine fidelity, compare replayed state transitions. Different measurements answer different questions.

```python
predictions = {
    "critic_problem": "larger value error on beatdown",
    "execution_problem": "more delayed or lost actions",
}
print(len(set(predictions.values())))
```

It prints `2`. The predictions are distinct, which makes a diagnostic design possible. Merely renaming the same aggregate score would not create two tests.

A **negative control** is a case where the effect should not occur. A **positive control** is a case where a known effect should be detected. In software evaluation, a planted bug can serve as a positive control: deliberately wrong output should fail the checker.

An instrument that reports identical results for correct and deliberately wrong data is not reassuring. It may be blind. Check the instrument before interpreting a null result as absence of a problem.

## 4. Read intervals in relation to the claim

A **null result** is a result that does not establish the proposed effect under the chosen test. An interval spanning zero may be consistent with both a small benefit and a small harm. It is not proof that the true effect is exactly zero.

Suppose an estimated subgroup difference is -0.001 with interval [-0.043, 0.044]. The estimate is near zero, and the interval allows differences in both directions. This does not support a confident claim of a large negative subgroup gap.

```python
estimate = -0.001
low, high = -0.043, 0.044
print(low <= 0 <= high)
```

It prints `True`. If the claim were “the difference is below -0.10,” this interval lies entirely above that threshold and conflicts with that particular large-gap claim. A different claim, such as “the difference is below zero by any amount,” is not resolved as strongly.

A **practical margin** is the size of effect that matters for the decision. Predeclare it. Otherwise a researcher can move the goalposts after seeing the interval. Tests of equivalence require a suitable interval inside a chosen margin; failing to detect a difference is not automatically equivalence.

Always distinguish an individual metric from the broader capability. A critic can have similar AUC across groups and still have poor calibration in a particular phase. A diagnostic result narrows explanations rather than proving every component correct.

## 5. Correct the conclusion, then choose the next test

A **revision** changes a claim in response to evidence. Write what was previously believed, what was measured and what now follows. This makes the update auditable and prevents the old explanation from surviving in a later summary.

```python
revision = {
    "before": "a large beatdown ranking deficit explains the losses",
    "evidence": "subgroup AUC difference near zero in this test",
    "after": "the proposed large deficit is not supported here",
}
print(revision["after"])
```

It prints `the proposed large deficit is not supported here`. The revised sentence is narrower than “the critic is perfect.” It rejects an overbroad diagnosis without inventing a new certainty.

A **next test** should follow from the remaining uncertainty. If the subgroup critic explanation fails, inspect action timing, opponent coverage, policy response or another measured discrepancy. Do not change all of them at once and then claim to know which fix worked.

The project's no-discard approach distinguishes a failing candidate from a research goal. A candidate can fail a gate while the goal remains: understand the failure, change the responsible component and retest. Continuing the same run without changing the hypothesis is not the same as iterating intelligently.

Keep evidence grades attached to each step. “The earlier premise is contradicted” can be measured as a conclusion from a diagnostic. “A different timing problem is responsible” may still be plausible. Only a test that distinguishes that mechanism can upgrade it.

Finally, resist **selection bias**, the distortion created by choosing which evidence to inspect or report based on its result. If several seeds or subgroups were examined, report that scope. A striking single subgroup discovered after many comparisons deserves fresh confirmation.

## In the real project

From the dated critic diagnostic in `HANDOFF.md`:

```text
  - **u0250 (exact training mix)**: AUC 0.813 vs 0.814, diff -0.001 [-0.043, +0.044]; MSE diff -0.004 [-0.074, +0.061].
  - **u0065 (partial mix: evicted snapshots dropped)**: beatdown BETTER: AUC +0.052 [+0.014, +0.090], MSE -0.076 [-0.132, -0.018].
```

The first line gives the nearly equal AUCs and uncertainty for the exact training mix, plus a squared-error comparison. The second describes a different checkpoint and a partial mixture, so it must not be silently pooled with the first. Higher AUC and lower mean squared error are the favourable directions.

The same entry concludes:

```text
- (c) "the critic cannot judge beatdown games" is contradicted -> C2/F2 are unlikely to close the beatdown gap (they can only
  cut variance, and there is no extra variance there to cut). The running C2/F2 arms finish anyway (cheap; they test the
  variance effect on win rate overall).
```

Line one grades the earlier explanation as contradicted in that diagnostic context. Line two distinguishes the proposed mechanism from the remaining experiment. Line three states the narrower remaining question. These are historical report statements, not current runtime instructions, and print nothing.

The lesson does not infer that all future critic problems are impossible. The result applies to the measured checkpoints, mixture and diagnostics described in the entry.

## Practice plan

The exercises start with an exact count claim so the distinction between evidence and explanation is unambiguous. Next classify an interval relative to a predeclared effect threshold, using measured support, contradiction or unresolved status.

The third exercise checks an instrument against an intentionally incorrect rate that includes unknown outcomes in its denominator. The final exercise constructs a revision record that cannot omit either the old claim or its evidence.

Use these habits when writing your capstone. Every numerical statement should be recomputable. Every causal explanation should either cite a discriminating test or remain explicitly plausible. A concise qualified conclusion is more useful than a confident story that the available evidence cannot distinguish from alternatives.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a plausible cause measured because the outcome was measured.
- Reading an interval crossing zero as proof of no effect.
- Changing the practical margin after seeing results.
- Pooling results from different evaluation mixtures without disclosure.
- Replacing one contradicted explanation with an untested certainty.

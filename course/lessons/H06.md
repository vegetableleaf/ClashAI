## Why this matters for ClashBot

A ClashBot policy can become excellent at beating an opponent that never defends X-Bow correctly. That is a real improvement against that opponent and weak evidence about performance against humans. The training environment includes the opponent's decisions as well as the simulator's rules.

`scratchpad/gauntlet/L74/plan1026/opp/OPP_LEARNER.md` describes training the human-like opponent model for strength. This reverses the usual perspective: the learner in that experiment is the opponent, not the Icebow bot we ultimately want to improve.

The file records calibration arms, frozen output heads, drift concerns and later timing-head work. We will use those records as an example of why a single win-rate number is insufficient. The lesson's exercises manipulate small tables and probabilities rather than launching any league jobs.

## 1. An opponent is part of the experiment

**Self-play** means training against another version or copy of the agent. It can generate an unlimited curriculum of challenges, but two copies can develop habits that are useful only against each other. A **curriculum** is the sequence or distribution of tasks encountered during learning.

A fixed opponent provides a stable target. A changing opponent can remain challenging as the learner improves. Neither is automatically correct. With a fixed weak opponent, the learner may exploit one predictable opening. With a changing opponent, a score from yesterday may not be comparable with today's score.

```python
scores = {"fixed_opponent": [0.5, 0.7], "new_opponent": [0.5, 0.4]}
print(scores["fixed_opponent"][-1] - scores["fixed_opponent"][0])
```

It prints a floating-point value close to `0.2`. The increase describes improvement against the fixed opponent. It says nothing about the difficulty of the new opponent. Always record the opponent identity alongside the learner identity.

For Icebow, diversity matters because defending Hog pressure, surviving beatdown and answering siege demand different responses. A model that sees one deck repeatedly may learn a narrow policy even if the simulator contains every card.

## 2. Build a pool with an explicit sampling rule

A **league** is a collection of opponents and rules for choosing among them. A **snapshot** is a saved policy from an earlier point in training. Historical snapshots can keep the learner from forgetting how to beat older strategies.

A **mixture weight** is the probability assigned to an opponent or opponent category. The weights must be nonnegative and sum to one after normalization. Raw counts or priorities can be converted into probabilities by dividing by their total.

```python
weights = {"baseline": 2, "historical": 1, "specialist": 1}
total = sum(weights.values())
probabilities = {name: weight / total for name, weight in weights.items()}
print(probabilities)
```

It prints `{'baseline': 0.5, 'historical': 0.25, 'specialist': 0.25}`. Half the expected matches use the baseline. A zero-weight entry is allowed if intentionally disabled; a negative weight is not a probability.

The random seed makes a sampling sequence reproducible, but the pool contents must also be pinned. If the name `latest` resolves to a different checkpoint, the same seed no longer recreates the same experiment. Record stable identifiers and keep evaluation opponents fixed while comparing candidates.

A pool can be large yet strategically narrow. Ten copies of nearly the same policy do not provide ten independent styles. Inspect behaviour as well as membership count.

## 3. Use exploiters to reveal blind spots

An **exploiter** is a policy trained or selected to expose weaknesses in another policy. A specialist that punishes undefended X-Bows can reveal a vulnerability missed by an average opponent. It need not be a realistic model of the entire human population.

An **evaluation matrix** stores each learner's results against each opponent. Rows represent candidate learners and columns represent opponents. Looking only at an average can hide a disastrous column.

```python
candidate = {"hog": 0.7, "siege": 0.2, "beatdown": 0.6}
mean_score = sum(candidate.values()) / len(candidate)
print(round(mean_score, 2), min(candidate.values()))
```

It prints `0.5 0.2`. The mean is respectable for this toy, but siege performance is poor. A required subgroup threshold would catch that weakness.

**Non-transitivity** means there is no single ordering that predicts every matchup: A can beat B, B can beat C, and C can beat A. Such cycles are common in strategic games. A league should preserve relevant counterstrategies instead of keeping only the checkpoint with the highest score against one opponent.

Exploiters are diagnostic tools. Training exclusively against an unnatural exploiter can create another distribution shift. The final evaluation should cover the intended deployment population and keep the exploiter score as a separate diagnostic.

## 4. Strength and human-likeness are different axes

An opponent's **strength** measures how hard it is to beat under a specified test. Its **human-likeness** measures similarity to human behaviour. A strong but mechanically strange policy may be a useful stress test and a poor model of live opponents.

Part G introduced behavioural comparisons. Here we use a simple two-axis report: bot win rate against the opponent and a style distance from human data. Lower bot win rate means a stronger opponent in this specific matchup. Lower style distance means closer behaviour under that metric.

```python
arms = [
    {"name": "A", "bot_win": 0.85, "style_distance": 0.30},
    {"name": "B", "bot_win": 0.80, "style_distance": 0.31},
]
print(arms[1]["bot_win"] < arms[0]["bot_win"])
```

It prints `True`. Arm B is harder for the bot in this toy comparison. That is not automatically good news for the bot's own policy; it may be good news for opponent calibration.

Always state the seat whose wins are counted. A log headed `CLXIII W` counts CLXIII wins. Reading it as opponent wins reverses the conclusion. Also distinguish training reward against a sampled partner mixture from evaluation win rate against a fixed benchmark. They can move differently without a logging bug.

Freezing output-head weights does not guarantee fixed outputs if a shared encoder continues learning. H08 will examine that drift mechanism explicitly.

## 5. Read plateaus without inventing explanations

A **plateau** is a period with no meaningful improvement under a declared criterion. Define the metric, improvement threshold and observation window before deciding that a run has plateaued. A flat noisy trace is evidence about observed progress, not proof that learning is impossible.

```python
scores = [0.20, 0.24, 0.25, 0.25]
gain = max(scores[2:]) - max(scores[:2])
print(round(gain, 2))
```

It prints `0.01`. Whether one point matters depends on uncertainty and the predeclared threshold. Do not choose the window after seeing the curve merely to make the conclusion stronger.

The opponent document later identifies a specific limitation: earlier card/cell updates did not train timing through the policy objective. That is a measured mechanism supported by gradient checks, not just a story attached to a flat curve.

A **calibration arm** is one controlled training variant used to adjust a model toward a target. Compare arms under the same engine build, tables, opponent seat and evaluation seeds. Different hyperparameters can be the intended treatment; a simultaneous table change is a confound unless deliberately studied.

When reporting a pool score, choose its weighting rule explicitly. An equal average across decks answers a different question from a live-traffic-weighted average. Both can be useful. Report the subgroup results so the weighting cannot conceal a weakness.

## Common mistakes

- Reading bot wins as opponent wins.
- Changing pool contents while claiming a fixed evaluation.
- Using pool size as a substitute for behavioural diversity.
- Calling any short flat segment a plateau.
- Assuming a strong exploiter is a representative human opponent.

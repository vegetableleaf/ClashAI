## 1. Define the comparison

A **control** is the reference condition; a **treatment** is the changed condition. An **effect size** measures how much they differ. For win fractions, candidate minus baseline is an absolute difference in probability; multiply by 100 for **percentage points**. A rise from .50 to .55 is five percentage points and ten percent relative improvement. Those statements use different denominators. Name the winner perspective, opponent population, engine version, table version, and sample selection before calculating. Otherwise a precise difference can answer the wrong question.

```python
base,new = .50,.55
print(round(100*(new-base),1))
print(round(100*(new-base)/base,1))
```

**Output:** `5.0` and `10.0`.

The first output is percentage points; the second is relative percent. The practical importance depends on the task. A five-point live improvement would matter, but a five-point change against one frozen simulator opponent may not transfer to live play. A measured result needs a bounded conclusion.

## 2. Pair conditions before subtracting

A **paired design** evaluates two conditions on matched experimental units. In a simulator this may mean the same opponent deck and initial seed. **Common random numbers** means reusing a random stream across treatments to reduce irrelevant variation. It helps most when the shared randomness affects both similarly. Different actions can change how many draws an engine consumes, so identical seeds do not guarantee every later random event matches. Align pairs by identifiers before subtracting. If one side is missing, report the incomplete pair instead of quietly treating it as a loss.

```python
base = {11:0, 12:1, 13:0}
new = {13:1, 11:1, 12:1}
diffs = [new[s]-base[s] for s in sorted(base)]
print(diffs)
print(round(sum(diffs)/len(diffs),3))
```

**Output:** `[1, 0, 1]` and `0.667`.

Each difference is candidate minus baseline for the same seed. A tied pair contributes zero. Sorting makes the display deterministic; identifier matching makes the comparison valid. The mean difference can be compared with a paired uncertainty calculation, rather than pretending the two samples contain unrelated games.

## 3. Hypotheses and p-values

A **null hypothesis** is a specific reference claim, such as no systematic treatment advantage. A **test statistic** summarizes how incompatible the data looks with that claim. A **p-value** is the null-model probability of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. It is not the probability that the null is true, nor the probability that the result occurred by chance in an unrestricted sense. A small p-value can accompany a tiny practical effect. A large one may reflect little data. For a toy paired sign test, discard ties and count which side wins each remaining pair.

```python
from math import comb
# Toy: four non-tied pairs all favor the candidate.
n=4
one_tail=sum(comb(n,k)*.5**n for k in range(4,n+1))
p_two=min(1,2*one_tail)
print(p_two)
```

**Output:** `0.125`.

Under independent equally likely signs, all four favoring one side is still not very strong evidence. This exact two-sided sign calculation uses the symmetric binomial tails. Repeatedly trying many candidates and reporting only the best p-value makes false discoveries more likely. Freeze comparisons and report selection, as C07 develops.

## 4. Standardize behavioral differences

A **standardized difference** expresses a mean difference in units of a reference standard deviation. One teaching convention is d=(model_mean-human_mean)/human_sd. A production Cohen d calculation may use a pooled standard deviation instead; always read the implementation and state the convention. **Root mean square**, or RMS, squares the feature differences, averages them, and takes the square root. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from canceling. This is useful for a collection of behavior statistics, but correlated features can still make the summary overemphasize one behavior.

```python
from math import sqrt
model_mean,human_mean,human_sd = 12,10,4
d=(model_mean-human_mean)/human_sd
values=[d,-d]
rms=sqrt(sum(x*x for x in values)/len(values))
print(d)
print(rms)
```

**Output:** `0.5` and `0.5`.

The ordinary mean of .5 and -.5 is zero, hiding both differences. RMS preserves their size. A zero reference standard deviation needs a declared policy rather than division by zero. Our exercise uses positive supplied scales and labels its convention; it does not claim to reimplement the full human-likeness gate.

## 5. Distribution shape and ranking

An **empirical cumulative distribution function**, or ECDF, gives the fraction of observations at or below each value. The **Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance**, or KS distance, is the maximum absolute difference between two ECDFs. A **KS test** uses that distance with sample sizes and assumptions to assess a distributional null; the distance alone is not a p-value. **AUC**, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, also has a ranking interpretation: the chance a randomly chosen positive example scores above a negative, counting ties as half. Here positive can mean human, provided that convention is stated.

```python
a,b=[1,2],[2,3]
points=sorted(set(a+b))
d=max(abs(sum(x<=t for x in a)/len(a)-sum(x<=t for x in b)/len(b)) for t in points)
pos,neg=[.8,.5],[.5,.2]
auc=sum((p>n)+.5*(p==n) for p in pos for n in neg)/(len(pos)*len(neg))
print(d)
print(auc)
```

**Output:** `0.5` and `0.875`.

The four ranking pairs earn 1,1,.5,1 points. High AUC means separable populations under this score, not good human likeness. Evaluate a learned score on held-out data; otherwise it may memorize examples. Formal classifier training comes in Part F. Our exercises only calculate distances and ranking summaries from already supplied numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a p-value the probability that the hypothesis is true.
- Pairing games by row position when seed identifiers differ.
- Treating a nonsignificant difference as proof of equivalence.
- Ignoring ties in AUC.
- Reporting RMS d without saying which features and reference scale were used.

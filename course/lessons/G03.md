# G03 - Per-decision vs closed-loop evaluation

**Time:** about 4 hours ? **You will be able to:** separate replay prediction from complete-game behaviour and analyse the X-Bow defence gap.

## Why this matters for ClashBot

The siege study in `scratchpad/gauntlet/L74/plan1026/opp/siege/SIEGE.md` asks why imitation opponents let ClashBot's X-Bows lock onto towers much more often than live humans do. Some per-decision prediction metrics looked reasonable, yet complete simulator games remained very different.

A **per-decision evaluation** scores predictions on recorded observations one decision at a time. A **closed-loop evaluation** lets a policy act, lets the environment change, and feeds the resulting observations back into the policy. The action influences the next input.

The source calls these level A and level B in the opponent workflow. Good level-A results do not logically imply good level-B results. This distinction is essential when a simulator opponent is used to train the Icebow learner through reinforcement learning later in the course.

## 1. Recorded states keep the path fixed

In replay evaluation, the next observation comes from the recording. Even if your predicted card is wrong, the next row still follows the human's actual action. The model is not forced to live with its mistake.

```python
labels = [0, 1, 1, 2]
predictions = [0, 0, 1, 2]
accuracy = sum(a==b for a,b in zip(labels,predictions))/len(labels)
print(accuracy)
```

This prints `0.75`. It tells us three labels matched. It says nothing about whether the second error would have caused a tower to fall or merely changed an unimportant cycle card.

A replay task is valuable because it isolates prediction quality on a common set of observations. It supports comparisons of accuracy, NLL, and calibration. Its limitation is that the observations are supplied by somebody else's trajectory.

A **trajectory** is an ordered sequence of observations and actions through a match. In closed loop, the evaluated model helps create that trajectory.

## 2. Actions change future inputs

A defensive Tesla consumes elixir and changes which units survive. Waiting leaves elixir available but may allow an X-Bow to begin damaging a tower. The next decision therefore depends on previous choices.

A small teaching environment can make the feedback visible. Let threat begin at zero. Waiting increases threat by one. Defending reduces it by two, down to zero. After either action, threat at least three removes one tower HP.

```python
threat = 0
hp = 5
for action in ["wait", "wait", "wait"]:
    threat = threat+1 if action=="wait" else max(0, threat-2)
    if threat >= 3:
        hp = max(0, hp-1)
print(threat, hp)
```

This prints `3 4`. The third wait changes not only a prediction score but the next physical state. This is a teaching state machine, not a model of Clash Royale's combat rules.

In a real simulator such as RoyaleSim or crforge, effects include movement, targeting, damage, deployment delay, and many more interactions. The same feedback principle holds even though the state transition is much richer.

## 3. Errors can compound

**Compounding error** occurs when one mistake changes the state in ways that make later mistakes more likely or more costly. The policy may encounter situations uncommon in its demonstration data.

As a rough illustration, suppose each of ten critical decisions independently succeeds with probability 0.9. The probability all ten succeed is 0.9 to the tenth power.

```python
per_decision = 0.9
count = 10
print(round(per_decision**count, 3))
```

This prints `0.349`. It is an illustration under an independence assumption, not a formula converting card accuracy into match win rate. Real decisions are dependent, recoveries occur, and many errors have little consequence.

The lesson is to resist an unjustified translation such as ?90% accurate means a 90% strong opponent.? Match success depends on which decisions are wrong, how errors interact, and whether the environment exposes failure modes absent from recorded rows.

## 4. Define the event being measured

The siege source defines an X-Bow lock operationally using princess-tower damage within a time window after a reach X-Bow. An **operational definition** states exactly how a quantity is measured from available observations.

For a teaching function, suppose a lock means at least 5% of starting tower HP is lost within thirty seconds. Both the damage threshold and the time boundary must be explicit.

```python
damage_fraction = 0.06
elapsed_seconds = 12
locked = damage_fraction >= 0.05 and elapsed_seconds <= 30
print(locked)
```

This prints `True`. The exercise will use already computed booleans rather than reconstructing the reader's full event detector.

An **instrument** is the process that turns events into measurements. Live sightings can be delayed; simulator logs can expose exact events. Differences in instruments can resemble differences in opponent behaviour. Controls are needed before assigning a cause.

## 5. Break a pooled gap into interpretable pieces

The source groups responses as on-time troops, on-time buildings, late answers, elsewhere-only answers, and no plays. A **response-class mix** is the fraction of cases in each group. A **within-class lock rate** is the lock frequency among cases of one response type.

The overall lock rate is the weighted sum of within-class rates. Two populations can differ because they choose different response types, because the same types are less effective, or both.

```python
mix = {"on_time": 0.75, "late": 0.25}
rates = {"on_time": 0.4, "late": 0.8}
pooled = sum(mix[name]*rates[name] for name in mix)
print(round(pooled, 2))
```

This prints `0.5`. Changing the mix to more late responses raises the pooled rate without changing either class's effectiveness.

**Standardisation** here means recomputing one population's outcome using another population's class weights. It answers a descriptive question: what pooled rate would these conditional rates produce under that mixture? It does not by itself prove a causal effect of changing a response.

In the source's ftR breakdown, substituting the live class mix leaves much of the lock-rate gap. That points attention toward within-class effectiveness rather than only response frequency.

## 6. Read the siege evidence in its revised form

The initial aggregate level-A results did not support a broad claim that the model simply forgot to answer X-Bows. Card prediction and timing in the threat windows were not dramatically worse than elsewhere.

The later analysis refined that conclusion. Some X-Bow-specific counters were under-selected in relevant windows even when aggregate metrics looked acceptable. Closed-loop placements and follow-up commitment also differed. ?The model knows when to act? is therefore not equivalent to ?the resulting defence works.?

The source's same-environment comparison used another opponent under matched engine conditions. It found more human-like answer effectiveness for some cards, weakening an explanation that blamed only the simulator. It still recorded a remaining possibility: different policies may create different states before answering.

This is a good example of scientific revision. A broad hypothesis can be contradicted while a narrower card-specific mechanism remains supported. Do not freeze the lesson at the first table when the same document contains later controls.

## 7. Compare complete runs fairly

Use the same engine build, game rules, observation processing, opponent deck selection, and decoding settings when comparing policies. Otherwise a policy change is mixed with environmental changes.

A **paired difference** compares candidate and baseline on a shared experimental unit, such as the same scenario seed and deck pair. Pairing can reduce noise when both runs face similar difficulty.

```python
baseline = [0, 1, 0, 1]
candidate = [1, 1, 0, 1]
differences = [c-b for b,c in zip(baseline,candidate)]
print(differences)
print(sum(differences)/len(differences))
```

This prints `[1, 0, 0, 0]` and `0.25`. The average improvement is 0.25 on this tiny teaching sample. It is not a persuasive confidence interval.

Pair by an explicit identifier, not just file row order. Runs can finish in different orders. Reject mismatched IDs or report unmatched records separately. A missing result must not quietly become a loss.

For real match-log rates, G01's cleaning rule still applies: outcomes absent from derived are unknown, excluded from win-rate denominators and reported separately. Pairwise comparisons need both sides observed; disclose incomplete pairs instead of inventing outcomes.

## 8. Debug the loop in layers

When closed-loop behaviour differs, inspect whether proposed actions are legal and accepted. Then inspect timing, coordinates, and state transitions. Finally inspect strategic patterns such as repeated failed X-Bow attempts or elixir commitment.

A model may select the intended card but place it on a less effective cell. Top-one card accuracy would miss that. A correct cell label may still be decoded under the wrong coordinate convention. A good decoded action may arrive too late through the emulator path.

These layers suggest targeted checks rather than one giant win-rate test. Use synthetic cases for mechanics and real trajectories for end-to-end claims. A tiny state machine passing its checks does not validate crforge combat or a live phone reader.

## 9. What the exercises establish

The first exercise implements the illustrative all-correct probability with explicit assumptions. The second lets a policy interact with a tiny environment, so earlier actions genuinely alter later inputs. The third standardises conditional lock rates using declared response shares. The fourth pairs binary outcomes by scenario ID.

For the rollout task, the policy receives current threat and HP on every step. It returns wait or defend. Record the resulting state after each step and stop when HP reaches zero. A test compares an always-wait policy with one that defends when threat is at least two.

For the standardisation task, missing classes are errors, not zero lock rates. If you lack a conditional rate for a class present in the reference mix, you cannot calculate that counterfactual mixture honestly.

After finishing, explain why both evaluation levels remain necessary. Replay prediction isolates what the model learned from demonstrations; closed-loop evaluation tests the consequences of using those predictions. Neither replaces the other, and neither alone proves the bot's live performance.

## Common mistakes

- Turning per-decision accuracy directly into win probability.
- Ignoring the independence assumption in the compounding illustration.
- Attributing the whole gap to response mix without within-class checks.
- Pairing runs by completion order.
- Quoting only the earliest hypothesis while omitting later refinements.

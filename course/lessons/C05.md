## 1. Sample and population

A **sample** is the observed set of games. A **population** is the broader collection of games your claim concerns. A **point estimate** gives one number for a population quantity; here p-hat = wins/n. The hat means estimated, not exact. The observed fraction is exact arithmetic for the recorded decisive outcomes, subject to correct data cleaning. Its interpretation as a future win probability requires assumptions about how those games were selected and whether conditions remain comparable. A display rounded to one decimal percentage point loses a little arithmetic precision but does not create the main uncertainty.

```python
w,n = 398,480
p = w/n
print(round(p,6))
print(f'{100*p:.1f}%')
```

**Output:** `0.829167` and `82.9%`.

These describe the same sample. They do not mean exactly 82.9 of every hundred future games will be wins. Keep the count and denominator in reports so readers can recompute the fraction and understand evidence volume. Unknown outcomes remain separate, as taught in C02.

## 2. Standard error

The **standard error** is the typical variation of an estimator across repeated samples under a model. For independent Bernoulli trials, the estimated standard error of a proportion is sqrt(p-hat*(1-p-hat)/n). It differs from the standard deviation of the count in C04: one is in probability units, the other in wins. An increase from 100 to 400 games roughly halves the standard error, rather than reducing it fourfold. This is why detecting small improvements can require many matches. The expression is undefined for n=0 and suspiciously zero at all wins or all losses.

```python
from math import sqrt
p=.5
print(round(sqrt(p*(1-p)/100),3))
print(round(sqrt(p*(1-p)/400),3))
```

**Output:** `0.05` and `0.025`.

A naive interval p-hat plus or minus 1.96 standard errors is a large-sample approximation. It can extend outside zero to one and collapse at extreme samples. We introduce it to explain the idea, then use Wilson for the exercises. A standard error cannot account for an unrecorded software change or biased missing outcomes.

## 3. Wilson confidence intervals

A **confidence interval** comes from a procedure designed to cover the fixed population value at a stated rate across repeated sampling. For a 95% procedure, about 95% of intervals would cover that value under its assumptions. It is not a probability assigned to the fixed parameter after observing one interval. The **Wilson interval** adjusts both centre and width to behave better for small samples and extreme proportions. z=1.96 is the conventional normal reference value for an approximately 95% two-sided interval. The formula uses only arithmetic and square root.

```python
from math import sqrt
w,n,z = 5,10,1.96
p=w/n
den=1+z*z/n
centre=(p+z*z/(2*n))/den
half=z*sqrt(p*(1-p)/n+z*z/(4*n*n))/den
print(round(centre-half,3),round(centre+half,3))
```

**Output:** `0.237 0.763`.

den is a shared denominator. centre is adjusted toward the middle for small samples. half is the interval's half-width. The broad interval is appropriate for only ten games. Return None when there are no decisive games; an invented interval would conceal the missing evidence. Clamping tiny floating-point overshoots at zero and one is harmless.

## 4. Clopper-Pearson and exact tails

The **Clopper-Pearson interval** inverts binomial tail probabilities. It is often called exact because it uses the binomial model rather than a normal approximation. Exact does not mean shortest, and its coverage is generally conservative: at least the nominal level under the model. For a two-sided 95% interval, each tail uses alpha/2=.025, where **alpha** is the error rate .05. For w>0, the lower endpoint solves P_p(X>=w)=.025; for w<n, the upper endpoint solves P_p(X<=w)=.025. At w=0 the lower endpoint is zero, and at w=n the upper endpoint is one.

```python
n=10
alpha=.05
upper = 1-(alpha/2)**(1/n)
lower = (alpha/2)**(1/n)
print(round(upper,3))
print(round(lower,3))
```

**Output:** `0.308` and `0.692`.

These are exact-model boundary examples: zero wins in ten has upper bound about .308; ten wins in ten has lower bound about .692. Interior endpoints can be found by repeatedly bisecting a probability interval and evaluating the C04 binomial sum. This lesson uses small boundary cases without adding an unlisted scientific package dependency.

## 5. Thresholds and sample planning

A **decision threshold** is a criterion chosen before viewing the result. The phrase CI low > 32.7% means the lower bound, not just the point estimate, must strictly exceed .327. Specify whether the interval is one-sided or two-sided; our exercises use the two-sided Wilson lower bound. A rough worst-case planning formula for a single proportion's margin m is n approximately z squared times .25 divided by m squared. **Margin** means half-width here. This is not a power calculation for comparing two bots. A comparison needs a target effect size, error rates, and a paired or independent design, introduced in C06.

```python
from math import ceil
z=1.96
for margin in [.10,.05]:
    print(ceil(z*z*.25/(margin*margin)))
print(.327 > .327)
```

**Output:** `97`, `385`, and `False`.

ceil rounds upward to a whole number of games. Halving the desired margin roughly quadruples n. These planning numbers assume independent trials with a stable population and complete outcomes. Repeatedly checking and stopping when an interval happens to clear a bar changes the procedure; freeze the stopping plan as well as the threshold.

## Common mistakes

- Saying 398/480 is an uncertain arithmetic calculation rather than an uncertain population estimate.
- Interpreting a 95% confidence interval as a 95% posterior probability.
- Using a zero standard error at all wins as proof of certainty.
- Treating repeated windows or paired games as independent observations.
- Using a missing denominator as zero percent.
